01 Foundations

What a lubricant really does inside a machine.

A lubricant is not just a slippery fluid. In real machinery it creates a film, reduces friction, carries away heat, controls wear, suspends contaminants, protects against corrosion and supports reliability. The correct product is the one that matches the duty cycle, not simply the one with the most familiar grade.

Film formation separates moving surfaces to reduce friction and wear.
Heat removal carries thermal load away from critical zones.
Cleanliness transports contaminants and helps keep systems clean.
Protection supports corrosion resistance and component life.
A practical lubricant discussion usually starts with speed, load, temperature, contamination, drain expectations and OEM guidance.

02 SAE J300 engine-oil grades

How to read 0W-20, 5W-30, 10W-40, 15W-40 and 20W-50.

The number before the W speaks to low-temperature behaviour and cold-start flow. The second number describes viscosity at operating temperature. Together they help explain start-up performance, film thickness, efficiency and protection under load.

0W-20 very low viscosity with excellent cold-start flow and efficiency.
5W-30 low viscosity for broad everyday protection.
10W-40 balanced grade with wider temperature protection.
15W-40 / 20W-50 stronger film for heavier duty or severe conditions.
03 Industrial oils and ISO VG logic

Industrial viscosity selection is about machine condition and duty.

For industrial equipment, the conversation often shifts from SAE to ISO VG. Hydraulic systems, gearboxes, bearings and compressors all rely on the right viscosity range, but the final choice depends on load, speed, temperature, contamination and equipment design.

VG 32 / 46 / 68 commonly associated with hydraulics and general industrial systems.
VG 150 / 220 / 320 more common in higher load gear and bearing environments.
Too thin may reduce film strength and increase wear risk.
Too thick can create drag, heat and flow limitations.
04 Grease fundamentals

Grease selection depends on thickener, base oil and operating condition.

Grease is not just “thick oil”. It is a lubrication system built from base oil, thickener and additives. The correct grease depends on bearing speed, load, temperature, contamination, relubrication interval and sealing conditions.

Base oil provides the actual lubricating film once it is released into the contact zone.
Thickener acts like the sponge or structure that holds the oil in place.
NLGI consistency describes how soft or firm the grease is for the application.
Application fit depends on speed, load, water exposure, dust and relubrication practice.

05 Base-oil foundation

Base oil sets the platform on which the finished lubricant is built.

When technical stakeholders ask about lubricant quality, base oil is one of the smartest places to begin. It influences oxidation stability, volatility, low-temperature flow, cleanliness and the overall room available for formulation design.

Group I more traditional mineral base oil, with wider solvency but lower performance versus later groups.
Group II / III cleaner and more stable base stocks commonly used in many modern formulations.
Group IV PAO-based synthetic platform known for strong low-temperature and oxidation behaviour.
Group V specialty base stocks used for targeted formulation benefits or niche performance needs.

06 Heavy-duty diesel oils

HD oils have to do more than lubricate.

In heavy-duty engines the lubricant also manages soot, deposit control, oxidation, acid neutralisation and cleanliness. That is why additive balance matters so much. A fleet-facing or industrial discussion becomes stronger when the duty profile is translated into lubricant function.

Detergency and dispersancy help manage deposits and soot.
Film strength supports wear control at load-bearing interfaces.
Thermal stability matters in high-load and long-duty cycles.
Drain confidence improves when the oil stays healthy in service.

07 Marine lubrication

Marine systems create a more layered lubrication discussion.

Marine lubrication is not one universal product conversation. Trunk piston engines, crosshead engines, cylinder oils, system oils, hydraulic systems, gears and compressors all bring different functional demands. Understanding that architecture makes commercial conversations more credible.

Trunk piston engines require crankcase oils that support wear, detergency and oxidation control.
Crosshead engines separate system lubrication and cylinder lubrication roles.
Shipboard systems include hydraulics, gears and compressors, each with its own viscosity logic.
Marine credibility improves when the system is explained clearly, not just the product name.
08 AdBlue / DEF basics

AdBlue is not a lubricant, but it matters in heavy-duty fleet conversations.

AdBlue, also known as Diesel Exhaust Fluid (DEF), supports SCR after-treatment systems in many modern diesel vehicles. Commercially, it often sits next to lubricant conversations because customers want one partner who understands both machine protection and emissions-support products.

What it is a 32.5% high-purity urea solution in demineralised water.
What it does helps SCR systems reduce NOx emissions in the exhaust stream.
Clean handling contamination control matters because product purity is critical.
Storage thinking suitable packaging, cleanliness and temperature awareness support reliable use.
32.5%high-purity urea

11 Practical selection framework

A simple way to discuss lubricant selection with technical confidence.

When the product range gets wide, a structured selection flow keeps the conversation useful. The right lubricant is usually the result of a logical sequence: understand the machine, understand the duty, narrow the viscosity, then validate against the application environment and OEM direction.

Step 1 define machine type and lubrication point.
Step 2 map speed, load, temperature and contamination.
Step 3 determine suitable viscosity window and formulation needs.
Step 4 align with OEM guidance, economics and monitoring plan.
